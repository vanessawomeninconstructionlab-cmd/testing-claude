The one-sentence ICP

Who you're actually building for

Say it in a single breath — if you can't, the product gets fuzzy and the marketing gets weak.

A residential remodeling business owner doing $500K–$2.5M a year, running 3–8 long jobs at once with a small crew, who still answers their own phone — and whose homeowners are living in the mess, anxious, and texting them for updates all day.

Notice what's not in there: not "all contractors," not "construction." The pain you're curing only gets sharp when four things line up — long jobs, an anxious homeowner living on-site, multiple projects at once, and an owner who is the bottleneck. Remove any one of those and the pain gets mild. Your job is to find the person where all four are true.

Make it a person

Meet Dana

A named persona keeps every future decision honest — "would Dana actually tap this?"

D

Dana Reyes

Owner, Reyes Kitchen & Bath Remodeling · 44 · runs a 6-person crew

Dana was a project lead for a big remodeler for 12 years before going out on her own 4 years ago. She's got 5 kitchen and bath jobs running right now, all in occupied homes. She's on a jobsite by 7am, drives between three of them a day, does estimates at night, and invoices on Sunday. She has no office manager. Her phone is her business — and it will not stop buzzing. Half the buzzes are homeowners asking "is someone coming today?" and "how's it looking?" She loves the craft and hates feeling behind. Her whole pipeline is referrals, so looking disorganized isn't annoying — it's a threat.

The pain, in her words

Why Dana feels this more than anyone

This is the emotional core. Sell the feeling, not the feature.

"I spend more time telling people what I did than doing it. Every homeowner thinks they're my only job. And if I don't text back fast, they think I've disappeared — even though I was at their house yesterday."

Long jobs = sustained anxiety

A 10-week kitchen means 10 weeks of a homeowner with no sink, wondering every single day if progress is being made. That's weeks of "any update?" — not a one-time question.

They're living in it

The homeowner is stepping over drop cloths to make coffee. The emotional temperature is high. Silence reads as abandonment.

She's the only bottleneck

No project manager to absorb the texts. Every interruption lands on the person who's also trying to hang cabinets. The cost of the problem is her time and focus.

Referrals are the whole business

Residential lives and dies on word-of-mouth. "She kept me in the loop the whole time" is the review that gets her the next three jobs. Communication IS her marketing.

Focus discipline

Who is not your client

Saying no is how the product stays sharp. Every one of these looks tempting and will pull you off course.

Not this Production home builders

Building new houses nobody lives in yet. Homeowner isn't anxious and on-site — and they already run Buildertrend or Procore.

Not this Handyman & quick-fix trades

Jobs done in a day. No sustained anxiety, no ongoing update loop to solve. Nothing to keep them subscribed.

Not this Large GCs & commercial

They have PMs and admin staff whose literal job is client comms. Their "homeowner" is a facilities manager on a different wavelength.

Not this Emergency plumbing / HVAC

Same-day dispatch-and-done. That's the first-to-accept app you set aside — a different product, a different buyer.

The competition

What Dana uses today (this is your real rival)

You're not competing with fancy software. You're competing with a group text and her memory.

Group texts & phone calls Real incumbent

The default. Messy, easy to lose track of, and it's exactly the thing generating the interruptions. Your wedge is "same simplicity, but it works for you."

CompanyCam

Great for jobsite photos — but it's built for the crew and the office, not for keeping the homeowner calm. There's an opening for a homeowner-facing update.

Buildertrend / Jobber / Houzz Pro

Full project-management suites. Powerful, but heavy and pricey — Dana signs up, gets overwhelmed, and quits. Your advantage is doing one thing she'll actually keep using.

The strategic read: the big tools lose small remodelers to complexity. You win by being the thing she can set up in 10 minutes and her crew adopts without training.

Money

Will she pay — and how much?

Anchor the price against what the problem costs her, not against other apps.

Realistic price

$49–$99/month per company, flat. Charge the business, not per crew member — you want the whole crew posting updates, so never make more users cost more.

The value anchor

One referral job is $20K–$40K. "She kept me updated the whole time" earns that review. Framed against a single won job, $79/mo is a rounding error — say it that way.

Don't sell "save time." Sell "look like the most on-top-of-it contractor your client has ever hired." That's what she'll pay for.
Your unfair advantage

The women-owned distribution wedge

You don't just have a product idea — you have a warm audience most founders would kill for.

Your first 10 customers are the hardest to get. You already have a room full of them: women-owned remodeling firms in your WCL network. That's your beachhead — launch to them, get them using it, collect their words, and let their before/after posts ("here's how I keep my clients in the loop") become your marketing. "Built by a woman in construction, for women running remodeling businesses" is a positioning no incumbent can copy. Broaden to all remodelers later; start where you're already trusted.

From profile to product

What "truly serves" Dana actually requires

The ICP isn't decoration — it dictates the v1. These features fall directly out of who she is.

Make or break The 10-second crew update

A tired framer with dirty gloves, one hand free, standing in a gutted kitchen, must be able to post one photo + one line in seconds — no login maze, no training. If the crew won't do it, the whole product dies. This is your north star.

Homeowner gets a text, not an app

The homeowner should NOT have to download anything. They get a branded text and a simple web link to a clean project timeline. Zero friction on their side.

"Next up" beats the anxiety

Every update carries a one-line "coming next" ("painting tomorrow"). That single line is what actually stops the "any update?" text — it answers the question before it's asked.

One dashboard, all jobs

Dana sees her 5 projects at a glance and which ones have gone quiet — so she looks on top of every one without visiting each.

It carries her brand

Her name and logo on every homeowner update. It should make her look professional — that's the emotional payoff she's buying.

Before you build anything

3 questions to validate Dana is real

Ask 10 remodelers these — for the price of coffee — before you spend a dollar on developers.

  • "In a typical week, how many times does a client text or call just asking for a status update?" — If the number's low, you've got the wrong client. If they sigh and say "constantly," you found Dana.
  • "What do you use right now to keep clients in the loop?" — Listen for "nothing" or "just text them." That's the gap. If they name a tool they love, dig into what it's missing.
  • "If something cut those update texts in half and made you look more professional, what would that be worth per month?" — Their number tells you your price and whether the pain is real.
